# Synthesize

Help the reader understand what the material changes about a problem, why that judgment follows, and what can be reused. Do the essential selection and reasoning in the text so the reader does not have to reconstruct it from scattered notes or source links.

## Choose the question worth answering

Infer the reader and purpose from the request and context. Ask only when the missing distinction would materially change the content. Accept a single fragment of thinking as well as multiple documents; no project, Spec, or Run is required.

Work from the supplied or task-relevant material. Identify the problem, the earlier understanding or alternatives when known, and the uncertainty that matters now. Select content for its effect on understanding, a decision, or future work. Effort spent and chronological order alone do not determine importance. Retain a negative result, contradiction, or limitation when omitting it would change the meaning.

Adapt the emphasis to the use:

- For organizing ideas, connect the objective, assumptions, tensions, and options; distinguish a useful direction from an untested explanation.
- For an experiment summary, explain what comparison was made, whether it was valid, and how far it supports or challenges the research claim.
- For research notes, preserve changes in understanding, plausible mechanisms, counterexamples, and methods that may help with related problems.
- For a weekly report, connect meaningful progress, findings, and decisions to the goal and the next work. Explain a method only as far as the reader needs to understand that progress.

These are choices about content, not mandatory sections. The same evidence must retain the same factual meaning across audiences.

## Build the relationships between evidence and judgment

Read source material before interpreting it. Identify which observations support each other, answer different parts of the question, conflict, or cannot yet be compared. Check the conditions that determine the relationship, such as evaluation criteria, data, baseline, or operating assumptions. Do not average incompatible results or select only the most favorable evidence.

Explain the change in understanding when the material supports one: what was learned, which interpretation became more or less credible, which option was excluded, and what remains unresolved. If the notes show only completed work or an unchanged uncertainty, describe that progress accurately; do not manufacture a discovery.

Distinguish an observation from its interpretation and from a proposed action. A source author's conclusion is a claim to assess against its evidence. If results differ, explain an established reason when available; otherwise retain the disagreement and identify what would distinguish plausible explanations. Do not invent a causal story to make the summary feel complete.

Make the connection to the decision explicit. Show why the evidence matters for the current choice and how an unresolved question motivates any proposed next step. Keep an already recorded decision separate from your recommendation. Insufficient evidence for a benefit does not establish that a method can never work.

## Extract a method that can travel

Look for a reasoning move that could improve a later task: how to identify an effective comparison, locate the uncertainty that changes a choice, separate competing explanations, or decide when to stop investing. Preserve a lesson when it changes what someone would do in a similar situation.

Separate the case-specific conditions from the reasoning that produced a useful judgment. Ask which part still holds if the goal, data, or resource limit changes, and which part must be reconsidered. Use a counterexample or condition change from the material to challenge a candidate lesson; label unsupported extensions as suggestions to test.

Make it usable by explaining the situation in which it applies, the key action or judgment, why it helps, and the evidence or condition that would require adjustment. Choose the level of abstraction that permits reuse while retaining the conditions needed to judge it. Replace vague advice such as "improve experimental discipline" with the concrete decision it should change.

Match the strength of the lesson to the material. A single observation may support a local caution or a hypothesis; a transferable rule needs an argument for why its mechanism and conditions extend beyond that case. Do not require a new insight, universal method, or methodology section in every output. The method should first improve the synthesis itself.

For example, in an illustrative case where an old baseline and a candidate used different evaluation scripts, early scores do not establish a comparable gain. If the corrected comparison remains inconclusive, explain that limit. A useful conditional lesson is to check evaluation criteria and operating conditions before reusing a historical baseline, and perform a small calibration comparison when needed. This supports the credibility of a comparison; it does not prove that the candidate is ineffective elsewhere. Use real results and sources in an actual report.

## Write so the reader can follow the conclusion

Lead with the main understanding, then give the facts and reasons needed to assess it. Put the key evidence in the prose; use precise source links for traceability, not as a substitute for explaining the result. Preserve values, units, technical identifiers, conditions, and uncertainty when they change the conclusion.

Use natural language for the requested reader, stable terms, concrete actors and verbs, and connected sentences. Explain unfamiliar concepts in context. Remove repeated process details and empty claims such as "analyzed deeply" or "will optimize". Keep the specific change, rationale, and necessary next steps. A short sentence that omits the link between evidence and conclusion can increase reading effort.

Use paragraphs for reasoning, a table for comparisons, and a diagram when relationships are difficult to explain in text. Use an interactive explanation only when varying inputs helps understanding. Mark illustrations and simulated data clearly; they cannot replace measured evidence. Let the information determine the length and structure.

Before delivering, check whether the reader can understand the claim and its key reason from the text, and whether the retained lesson could guide a later choice within its stated conditions. Each passage should add a needed fact, relationship, or implication. Once evidence is explained, build the next judgment on it instead of repeating the same conclusion in the findings, next steps, and lessons. Keep only lessons that change a later choice; combine overlapping lessons and omit those that merely restate the result. Remove unsupported elevation and restore any omitted condition that could change the judgment.

## Keep the source facts intact

Deliver usable prose, rather than a promise to summarize or a plan for writing. When saving is requested or already part of the task, use the designated or existing note/report location. Preserve original Run facts and cite them; producing a summary does not create a new Run. New evidence-producing computations are separate work with their own authorization and evidence needs.

The main Agent integrates adopted project decisions into the relevant Spec under the original authorization. A note or report references that decision; it does not silently become a competing design owner. Drafting a Feishu weekly report produces ready-to-paste text. Sending it requires an explicitly authorized destination and action.
